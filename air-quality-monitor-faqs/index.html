Air Quality Monitor questions answered

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

The best air quality monitor for you depends entirely on what you hope to track and where you’ll use it. Most buyers will do fine with a mid-range model that measures PM2.5, CO2, temperature, and humidity; skip expensive lab-grade units unless you have a specific health need. Before you buy, decide whether real-time data, app connectivity, or a standalone display matters most to you.

What should I look for in an air quality monitor?

Start with the pollutants you care about. For general indoor air, you’ll want a monitor that detects particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), carbon dioxide (CO2), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), as well as temperature and humidity. Those five data points cover the majority of common concerns like stuffiness, allergens, and chemical off-gassing. Next, consider display and connectivity. A clear, easy-to-read screen is helpful if you don’t want to check a phone app every time. Many monitors also offer Wi-Fi or Bluetooth for remote logging and smart-home integration. Look for models that show both current readings and historical trends, since a single number doesn’t tell you whether the air is improving or getting worse over a few hours. Finally, think about sensor quality. While no consumer monitor is lab-grade, reputable brands typically use optical particle counters for PM and non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) sensors for CO2. Avoid very cheap units that rely on cheaper electrochemical sensors for CO2, which can drift significantly over time.

How accurate are consumer air quality monitors?

Consumer air quality monitors are not as precise as professional-grade instruments used by researchers or industrial hygienists, but they are accurate enough for everyday decision-making. The U.S. EPA and similar agencies have evaluated several popular models and found that many track PM2.5 within a reasonable margin of error when compared to reference monitors, especially at moderate pollution levels. Accuracy tends to suffer at very high or very low concentrations, and sensors can be affected by humidity or temperature extremes. That said, even a monitor with a 20 percent error rate is still useful for spotting trends like a sudden spike in particles after cooking or a gradual rise in CO2 during a meeting. Trust the relative changes more than the absolute numbers, and recalibrate if your model allows it (some have auto-calibration for CO2).

Do I need a monitor that measures PM2.5, or is CO2 enough?

It depends on your primary concern. Carbon dioxide (CO2) is an excellent proxy for ventilation, when CO2 rises above 1,000 ppm, it often means the room is stuffy and indoor pollutants may be building up. A CO2-only monitor can help you decide when to open a window or turn on a fan, which is valuable for offices and bedrooms. However, CO2 doesn’t tell you about airborne particles, which matter most for allergy and asthma triggers. PM2.5 sensors detect microscopic particles from smoke, dust, cooking, and outdoor pollution. If you live in an area with wildfire smoke or have a household member with respiratory issues, PM2.5 monitoring is arguably more important. The most useful monitors combine both sensors so you can see the full picture.

Where should I place my air quality monitor for the best readings?

Place the monitor at breathing height, roughly four to five feet off the ground, in a central spot away from windows, doors, direct sunlight, and obvious pollutant sources like a kitchen stove or an air purifier’s exhaust. Sensors near a window will give you a mix of indoor and outdoor readings, while placement right next to an air purifier will make the air look better than it actually is in the rest of the room. If you own multiple monitors, put one in the room where you spend the most time (often the living room or bedroom) and another in a space you’re curious about, such as a home office or basement. Many monitors can compare readings between rooms, which helps you understand where ventilation or filtration is needed most.

Can an air quality monitor help with allergies or asthma?

Yes, but only as a tool, not a cure. A monitor that tracks PM2.5 and PM10 can alert you when particle levels spike, for example, during vacuuming, cooking on a gas stove, or after a windy day when pollen blows in. Knowing that your indoor air is poor can prompt you to close windows, run an air purifier, or change HVAC filters sooner than you otherwise might. Asthma and allergy triggers can also include VOCs from paints, cleaning products, or new furniture. A VOC sensor helps you identify when chemical levels are elevated so you can ventilate the room. That said, no monitor can replace medical advice; it gives you data to make informed decisions about your environment, but it won’t diagnose symptoms.

How often should I calibrate or replace the sensors?

Most consumer air quality monitors require little to no user calibration for PM and VOC sensors. CO2 sensors, however, will drift over time and need periodic recalibration. Many models offer an automatic background calibration (ABC) that corrects readings based on the assumption that CO2 levels drop to near-outdoor levels (around 400 ppm) at least once a week. If you keep the monitor in a room that never gets good ventilation, this auto-calibration can actually make readings worse; in that case, look for a model that lets you disable ABC or manually calibrate with a reference instrument. For particulate sensors, the main issue is dust buildup on the fan and optics. Some monitors include a self-cleaning cycle, but you may need to gently clean the air intake every few months with a soft brush or compressed air. Electrochemical sensors (used in some budget CO monitors) have a finite lifespan of a few years, while NDIR sensors can last five years or more. Replace the entire unit or the sensor module when readings become erratic or the manufacturer recommends an end-of-life check.

Frequently asked questions

Will an air quality monitor detect mold in my home?

No consumer air quality monitor directly detects mold spores. However, a monitor that tracks humidity and temperature can indicate conditions favorable for mold growth, such as indoor humidity consistently above 60 percent. Combined with a VOC sensor, it may pick up the musty odors associated with active mold, but a visual inspection or professional mold test is the only reliable way to confirm mold.

Can I use an air quality monitor to check if my air purifier is working?

Absolutely. Place the monitor in the same room as your air purifier, away from the purifier’s intake or output. Run the purifier on its highest setting for a few hours and watch for a steady decline in PM2.5 numbers. A significant drop (for example, from elevated to low levels) confirms the purifier is effectively capturing particles. For gas-phase filters, a reduction in VOC readings can also indicate the purifier’s carbon filter is doing its job.

Why does my CO2 reading go up when I’m in a room with many people?

Humans exhale carbon dioxide, so when several people are in a poorly ventilated space, CO2 levels naturally rise. Readings above 1,000 ppm are common in occupied offices, classrooms, and meeting rooms. Concentrations above 2,000 ppm can cause drowsiness, headaches, and reduced cognitive performance. This is why CO2 is often called a proxy for ventilation quality: a high reading means the room isn’t getting enough fresh air.

What’s the difference between VOC and TVOC readings?

VOC typically stands for volatile organic compounds, and TVOC means total volatile organic compounds. Some monitors report a single number labeled VOC that actually represents total concentration. Others provide a TVOC value in parts per billion or an index. The distinction matters less than understanding the trend: a sudden spike usually indicates a source like cooking, painting, or new furniture. Both terms are used somewhat interchangeably in consumer devices, but check the manual for how your monitor reports them.

Do I need to be connected to Wi-Fi for the monitor to work?

Not for basic readings. Most air quality monitors will display real-time data on their screen without any internet connection. Wi-Fi or Bluetooth is typically required only to log historical data, receive alerts on your phone, or integrate with smart home systems like Alexa or Google Home. If you simply want to glance at the current air quality, a standalone monitor works fine even offline.

In shortChoosing an air quality monitor comes down to matching its sensor set to your specific worries, be it ventilation, particles, or chemicals. Look for clear trends over time rather than fixating on exact numbers, and place the monitor where you actually breathe. A well-chosen monitor won’t solve your air problems, but it will hand you the data you need to solve them yourself.
